My Urchin can sleep deeper, if I let it disconnect

November 11, 2026 embedded rust keyboards rmk bluetooth low-power 10 min read

In the previous post, my Urchin central reached about 62 µA asleep, with both Bluetooth connections retained. The displays were off, the low-frequency crystal was finally doing its job, and a keypress didn’t require a reboot and reconnection.

I still like that arrangement. Taking my hands off the keyboard for a minute shouldn’t mean asking all the Bluetooth devices to introduce themselves again.

But leaving it alone overnight is a different question. What would it cost to stop keeping those connections alive? And, rather more importantly, would it come back as a keyboard afterward?

The short answer is about 19.8 µA for the whole measurement board at a 4.0 V supply. A local keypress woke the central, it reconnected to the host and accepted keys, and I also checked its connection to the other half. It could then enter SYSTEM_OFF again and return to the same current floor.

That is enough to make me consider a second, longer idle timeout. It isn’t a production sleep policy yet.

This time, actually turn it off

The first post distinguished screen sleep from MCU System OFF. Until now, the firmware cleared the nice!view, cut its switched supply, and stopped periodic display work. The MCU still detected keys and maintained the host and split links.

For this experiment, I made a disposable central-only firmware snapshot. At the existing 30-second idle timeout, after the display had finished shutting down, it entered nRF52840 SYSTEM_OFF instead of remaining in connected sleep.

The normal application checkout stayed untouched. The snapshot started from this application revision, and kept its pinned RMK revision. I didn’t update the library in the middle of the power measurement. The diagnostic patch contains the temporary application changes, not a proposed general-purpose RMK shutdown API.

The PPK2 supplied the central through its battery connection at 4.0 V, with keyboard USB and the debugger disconnected. These are November 5 captures of the Urchin measurement board with its nice!nano v2, not isolated measurements of the nRF52840 supply. The same setup includes the board’s other components and whatever current they still draw while the MCU is off.

Throughout the measurements, I kept the supply at 4.0 V, which RMK reported as about 65% battery charge.

A key still needs a way in

SYSTEM_OFF is not a deeper version of waiting for the next Embassy timer. Nordic’s nRF52840 Product Specification v1.5, §5.3.3, describes the core being powered down and the system resetting when it wakes. GPIO DETECT is one of its wake sources.

The central’s matrix uses columns as outputs and rows as inputs. Before entering SYSTEM_OFF, the diagnostic drives all five columns high and configures all four rows as pulled-down inputs with high-level SENSE enabled. Closing a switch raises its row through the matrix diode and asserts DETECT.

The relevant row configuration is ordinary GPIO configuration, with one extra field:

w.set_dir(Dir::Input);
w.set_input(Input::Connect);
w.set_pull(Pull::Pulldown);
w.set_sense(Sense::High);

The GPIO configuration is retained in SYSTEM_OFF. No periodic matrix scan is needed to notice that level. I also replaced the running matrix wait’s SENSE settings and cleared stale latches, rather than assuming its interrupt-driven configuration was already the desired off-state configuration.

There is a little more to shutdown than writing the power register. The display finishes its final SPI transaction and loses its supply first. The diagnostic synchronously disables the Bluetooth controller, then masks interrupts and disables PPI channels so the running tasks cannot reconfigure the hardware. It stops any overlapping battery ADC conversion and waits for STOPPED, disables SPIM2, and clears RAM retention before calling embassy_nrf::power::set_system_off().

Nordic explicitly requires ongoing EasyDMA transactions to finish before SYSTEM_OFF. The ADC is worth remembering here: its 30-second sampling cadence can overlap the 30-second idle timeout.

This is a non-returning measurement path. It does not hand those peripherals back to their Rust owners and try to resume their futures. Wake starts the MCU again through reset and the installed bootloader. No RAM contents need to survive that trip.

A connected debugger also changes the experiment: the chip emulates SYSTEM_OFF to keep debug resources available. I measured with it disconnected, after a power cycle.

About twenty microamps, not zero

The first useful off selection was almost 30 seconds long:

Read the selected region: 19.79 µA over 29.43 seconds. The 1.30 mA whole-window average includes the earlier active period. Neither the height of the active peaks nor that mixed average is the off current.

The CSV from that capture has 10 µs sample spacing. Using a slightly different boundary, from 27 seconds to its end, gives 19.792 µA over 29.41 seconds. Moving the start to 30 seconds gives 19.791 µA; the 40–50 second interval gives 19.791 µA too.

The measurement summary records the source CSV filenames, hashes, and calculation windows for these captures.

That’s a stable result within this recording, not an uncertainty estimate accurate to a thousandth of a microamp. About 19.8 µA is the useful number.

Relative to the previously reported 62.07 µA connected-sleep selection, it is about 42.3 µA lower, or 68% less current. The important qualification is also the point of the experiment: the Bluetooth connections are no longer retained. These are different operating states and captures, not a controlled attribution of every saved microamp to one peripheral.

There is still a small repeating feature in the off trace:

The 115.3 ms quiet selection averages 19.56 µA. The complete visible window, including the small pulses, averages 19.79 µA. I kept the pulses in the reported off average.

Thresholding the CSV above 23 µA puts the pulses roughly 139 ms apart. I haven’t isolated their source. That spacing alone doesn’t make them Bluetooth events, firmware timer wakeups, or a particular board component. It also doesn’t make this a measurement of the SoC’s datasheet SYSTEM_OFF current.

The remaining whole-board current is a separate investigation. I don’t need to explain it away to find this reduction useful.

Coming back matters more than going away

I initially measured only the current after shutdown. No keypresses, no wake test. A low number from a board that never comes back would be easy enough to arrange.

The next recording started with the central off, included a local keypress and startup, then ended with another shutdown. The off selections on either side were 19.74 µA and 19.75 µA.

That established a repeatable electrical cycle. It didn’t establish a working Bluetooth keyboard. Host Bluetooth also misbehaved while I was trying to pair, so I kept the successful connection check separate from those first current captures.

Afterward, I verified that the central reconnected to the host and accepted keypresses. I also made sure it connected to the split peripheral half. The final recording captures the complete sequence:

Central at a 4.0 V supply. The displayed 999.21 µA average covers both active periods and startup, not just SYSTEM_OFF. The large peak sets the vertical scale; the two low-current intervals are not zero.

The final CSV gives:

Wake activity begins around 22.243 seconds. After startup, the one-second windows generally return to roughly 1.03–1.05 mA. The second shutdown is around 55.831 seconds. The two short off selections confirm the return to the earlier floor; they don’t replace the longer first capture as the main off-current measurement.

The first wake key is lost. It triggers a reboot, and the firmware doesn’t save or replay that event. RAM retention is disabled too. Subsequent keypresses worked after reconnection; I haven’t timed that reconnection or stress-tested repeated wakeups.

The tall startup peak isn’t the whole bill

One of the wake captures contains an exported peak of 128.10 mA. That certainly gets the plot’s attention.

The GUI startup selection contains 2.86 mC over 808.6 ms. A slightly wider CSV window, 4.95–5.78 seconds, contains 2.886 mC over 0.83 seconds. Neither selection identifies when Bluetooth reconnection completed.

Zooming into the peak makes the scale less alarming:

The selected 200 µs contains about 7.7 µC. That’s roughly 0.27% of the broader startup window’s charge, despite dominating its vertical scale.

I haven’t identified that peak’s source. I’m strictly reporting what the PPK2 export contains. No other claims are made.

The broader 2.886 mC startup selection is about 0.00080 mAh. That’s small enough to be encouraging. It is not a complete measurement of the additional cost of waking from SYSTEM_OFF: that would need an ordinary connected-wake comparison and a boundary that includes the full reconnection process. I won’t turn this selected window into a precise break-even timer.

Thirty seconds for the experiment, perhaps thirty minutes for use

I don’t want every short pause to reset the keyboard. The experiment used the existing 30-second timeout so I could measure it without waiting half an hour for every run.

The policy I’m considering is two-stage:

  • After a short idle period, keep today’s connected sleep: displays off, links retained, quick wake.
  • After perhaps 30 minutes without activity, enter SYSTEM_OFF and accept reset and reconnection on the next local keypress.[1]

For a deliberately simple eight-hour idle budget, using the earlier 62.07 µA connected-sleep result and this experiment’s 19.79 µA off result:

Hypothetical central idle policyCharge over eight hours
Connected sleep throughout0.497 mAh
30 minutes connected sleep, then 7.5 hours SYSTEM_OFF0.179 mAh
Difference0.317 mAh saved

With eight hours idle each day, that saves enough charge to power the central for another 0.31 hours, or about 18½ minutes, at the 1.03 mA awake-idle current measured in the previous post. Typing may draw a different current.

That’s about 64% less charge in that simplified overnight budget. It uses steady-state currents and leaves out the common initial awake period and the eventual wake/reconnection cost. It isn’t a measurement of an implemented 30-minute policy.

The daily budget is less dramatic

The previous post’s battery-runtime table was for continuous sleep. Its daily-use example, with four hours active and twenty hours in connected sleep, came to about 20.5 ideal days from the central’s 110 mAh battery.

Keeping those four active hours, but replacing eight of the connected idle hours with SYSTEM_OFF, gives:

StateHours per dayCurrentDaily charge
Active41.03 mA4.120 mAh
Connected idle1262.07 µA0.745 mAh
SYSTEM_OFF819.79 µA0.158 mAh
Total245.023 mAh

That’s 21.9 ideal days, compared with the earlier 20.5 days: about 1.4 more days per charge, or 6.7% longer runtime. The four active hours alone cost 4.12 mAh, about 82% of the new daily budget. Reducing overnight current can’t do much about that part.

This example assumes eight full hours in SYSTEM_OFF, unlike the overnight table’s half-hour connected grace period followed by 7.5 hours off. It still uses awake-idle current as the active-use estimate and excludes reboot and reconnection overhead.

Each half has its own 110 mAh battery. These numbers concern the central’s battery only; I can’t apply them to both halves or call the 68% sleep-current reduction a threefold increase in overall battery life. Awake time still matters, as do battery self-discharge, cutoff, and current changing as the battery voltage falls. A fixed 4.0 V measurement isn’t a battery rundown.

There is also a split-keyboard catch that matters more than another decimal place: the peripheral cannot wake a central whose BLE radio is off. This experiment wakes from a local central key. A usable two-half policy needs to make that behavior intentional, rather than surprise me when my first key after lunch happens to be on the right. I haven’t tested a SYSTEM_OFF image on the peripheral.

Where I stopped

This was a measurement spike, not a replacement for the connected sleep behavior I already like. The normal firmware remains unchanged, and there is no new 30-minute timer yet.

What I have now is a central that can reach about 19.8 µA whole-board current at 4.0 V, wake through a local keypress, reconnect to the host and the other half, accept typing, and go back to the same floor.

The earlier improvements made keeping the conversation going cheaper. This one asks whether, after enough silence, it’s worth hanging up.

I’m certainly considering it, but if my four-hour active-use estimate is correct, the extra 1.4 days per charge is close to negligible for me.

Footnotes

  1. If both halves enter SYSTEM_OFF, each needs its own local keypress to wake, reset, and reconnect. Neither half can wake the other over BLE while its radio is off. The central reconnects to the host, and the peripheral reconnects to the central. ↩
