My sleeping Urchin had a perfectly good crystal it wasn't using

November 4, 2026 embedded rust keyboards rmk bluetooth low-power 13 min read

In the previous post, I managed to stop blaming the displays for power that was going into a radio search. The screens slept, the halves stayed connected, and the peripheral settled at about 47 µA.

The central was still around 270–300 µA while sleeping with both the host and the other half connected. I’d enabled connection subrating, but hadn’t measured it yet.

This time I did. Then I removed a couple of things I didn’t need, tried a change that failed to save what I expected, and eventually noticed that the genuine nice!nano v2 had a perfectly good low-frequency crystal that my firmware wasn’t using.

The final central measurement was about 62 µA asleep, with both BLE connections retained, and about 1.03 mA awake without typing.

The quiet current between bursts barely moved. The bursts got cheaper.

The keyboard still has to be a keyboard

The target wasn’t the lowest number I could make the PPK2 display. I could get a lower number by disconnecting things. That wasn’t the keyboard I wanted to use.

My halves sleep through the night while connected to each other, and the central stays connected to the host. A keypress should wake the keyboard without making me wait for discovery and reconnection. This is connected idle, not MCU System OFF.

I used a partially populated Urchin measurement board with a genuine nice!nano v2 and one populated key for wakeup. The PPK2 supplied the central through its battery connection; keyboard USB was disconnected during current measurements. The peripheral had its own power.

The captures in this post are from October 31. They’re selected measurement windows, not a battery rundown test.

USB logging would have rather defeated that arrangement. Host-side Bluetooth capture, on the other hand, didn’t need another wire into the keyboard.

The host was Linux 7.2.11-arch1-1, with BlueZ 5.87-2. It also had my mouse and actual daily-use keyboard connected, so identifying the measurement keyboard mattered. So did keeping those packets out of the explanation for its current trace.

Subrating did help

The first useful correction was simple: I hadn’t actually measured the subrating-enabled firmware.

With it enabled on both halves, initial connected-sleep observations were around 117–124 µA. This one-minute screenshot averages 123.96 µA:

Already much better than 270–300 µA. The tall peaks are still there; their height isn’t the average.

Zooming in, I could select a quiet gap at about 30 µA, followed by short bursts reaching milliamps. Selecting only that gap would have made for a lovely sleep-current claim. It would also have left out most of what the keyboard was doing while asleep.

At first the spacing looked irregular:

This selection is deliberately burst-rich. Neither its 145 µA nor the quiet gap’s 30 µA replaces a representative whole-window average.

The CSV exports were more useful than eyeballing the screenshot. The PPK2 samples were 10 µs apart. Among the uneven gaps, there was a very regular train at approximately 225 ms: a good match for the split link’s factor-30 subrating on its 7.5 ms base interval. Other activity sat between those events.

Two links with different schedules don’t owe me one tidy pulse train.

A useful detour that wasn’t the goal

I powered off host Bluetooth with bluetoothctl power off, left the peripheral connected, and waited for the central’s host advertising to time out. That advertising session lasts five minutes; measuring the reconnection attempt would have answered a different question.

The result was 59.38 µA over a minute. But the 225 ms train was still there.

The pinned RMK implementation chose its sleeping split settings when sleep state changed. Losing the host while already asleep didn’t necessarily make it choose again. A keypress followed by another idle timeout did: with the host still off, the train became approximately 750 ms, and a 21.91-second capture averaged 44.60 µA.[1]

That was useful evidence about when the policy ran. It was not the answer to overnight connected consumption. I turned the host back on.

Easy to get distracted by a better number. Especially when the number is real, but belongs to a setup I don’t intend to use.

What did the host actually agree to?

Back in connected sleep, a steadier 30.72-second capture averaged 86.52 µA. Earlier windows had been higher, including occasional clusters of much more expensive activity. That variability was a reason to keep looking at charge and timing, not to pick whichever average made the next change look best.

I used btmon to record host HCI traffic:

sudo btmon -w urchin-connected-sleep.btsnoop

The measurement keyboard had its own connection handle. Once mapped to its address, the other keyboard and mouse could be excluded. There were no ACL packets for the measurement keyboard in that settled capture.

That did not mean its radio had stopped. Empty link-layer exchanges, retransmissions, and the separate split link aren’t exposed as ordinary application packets in this host capture. It only ruled out a stream of host-visible application traffic as the explanation for that window.

To learn the negotiated parameters, I started another capture before power-cycling the measurement central. Requests aren’t proof of acceptance; the successful completion events were the useful part.

The host link ended up at:

ParameterNegotiated value
Connection interval7.5 ms
Peripheral latency60
Supervision timeout6 seconds
TX / RX PHY2M / 2M

RMK first requested 15 ms with latency 30, then 7.5 ms with latency 60. Linux accepted both updates. This matched the roughly 457.5–465 ms spacing of the larger, host-like events in the current trace.

Stop computing a number I don’t use

My application explicitly started RMK’s words-per-minute processor. I don’t display WPM. I don’t use it for anything else either.

It still woke once a second to compute it.

Removing the task eliminated one of the one-second burst trains. The next capture averaged 85.27 µA, compared with 86.52 µA before. That’s a small difference, and these aren’t repeated controlled trials with an error bar. The clearer evidence was the missing train, not a claim that the subtraction gave its exact cost.

There was another one-second path. RMK’s USB LED-report reader tried to read from its HID OUT endpoint even with USB disconnected. A disabled-endpoint error led to a one-second wait and another attempt.

Embassy already has a way to wait until the endpoint is enabled:

self.hid_reader.ready().await;
self.hid_reader.read(&mut buf).await?;

The gate is endpoint readiness, not merely USB voltage being present. A charger can supply voltage without enumerating a keyboard.

With that wait added, the other one-second train disappeared. The capture averaged 85.51 µA, though, slightly higher than the WPM-only image. A larger burst cluster raised that window’s average. Again, the waveform supported removal of the repeated work; the total didn’t establish a precise saving for the one-line fix.

The USB readiness fix has a pending PR against RMK. It stays separate from the application clock choice. Tests exercise disabled-endpoint waiting, report delivery after enablement, disconnect/re-enable behavior, and preservation of other read errors. They don’t measure microamps.

Fewer events, more expensive events

The next trial increased host peripheral latency from 60 to 120 while asleep, restoring 60 on wake. The interval stayed at 7.5 ms, the supervision timeout at six seconds, and split settings stayed put.

The tempting calculation was: ordinary host-like events cost about 10–12 µC each; double their spacing and save roughly 11–13 µA. Peripheral latency permits skipping idle events; it doesn’t inherently force pending key data to wait for the full idle spacing.

Linux accepted latency 120. The event spacing did roughly double, from about 460 ms to about 910 ms.

The capture averaged 90.36 µA.

So much for that estimate.

Using the same threshold-based measurement on both exports, the median partial excess charge of ordinary host-like bursts increased from about 10.47 µC to 16.90 µC. There were also three expensive clusters in the new capture, compared with one in the USB-only capture.

The ordinary activity looked better between those clusters, but the clusters weren’t a spreadsheet error. The battery pays for them too. I couldn’t delete them from the average and call it a win.

The estimate had assumed nearly constant charge per event. The measurement had just told me that assumption was wrong.

The crystal was already on the board

One explanation worth checking was receive-window widening. If the clocks can drift while the devices skip events, the receiver needs timing allowance when it listens again. Longer gaps can mean more listening, not just fewer wakeups.

RMK’s generated nRF initialization selected the internal LFRC for MPSL and declared 500 ppm accuracy. Embassy’s default configuration also selected LFRC. That was a coherent configuration. It just wasn’t using the hardware this board had available.

The official nice!nano v2 schematic shows a populated 32.768 kHz crystal. No soldering required.

I changed the central to use it consistently in both Embassy and MPSL, leaving the latency-120 experiment in place. The MPSL configuration became:

nrf_sdc::mpsl::raw::mpsl_clock_lfclk_cfg_t {
    source: nrf_sdc::mpsl::raw::MPSL_CLOCK_LF_SRC_XTAL as u8,
    rc_ctiv: 0,
    rc_temp_ctiv: 0,
    accuracy_ppm: 50,
    skip_wait_lfclk_started: false,
}

Both RC calibration fields must be zero for a non-RC source. Embassy also needs LfclkSource::ExternalXtal; changing only the MPSL declaration isn’t the whole initialization.

The 50 ppm value is the crystal-clock reference used by Zephyr, not a tolerance I independently verified from the nice!nano crystal’s part number. The schematic confirms the crystal exists, not its worst-case accuracy across temperature and load. This is a tested configuration, not a new specification for the board.

I was surprised enough by the result to take a full minute: 70.77 µA. The host still reported 7.5 ms, latency 120, six-second supervision, and 2M PHY in both directions. The quiet floor was still about 29–30 µA.

That supports clock configuration as a useful improvement. It doesn’t identify every burst or prove receive-window widening caused every extra microcoulomb in the earlier capture. Crystal selection and the accuracy declaration changed together, and RC calibration was no longer required. I didn’t measure those effects separately.

Keep the board choice in the application

I briefly went down the route of adding configuration override hooks to RMK so the application could select both configurations while keeping generated startup.

Then I asked the simpler question: couldn’t the application already do this through #[Override(chip_init)]?

Yes. It could.

That’s where I left it. Urchin now uses the existing chip-init override on both halves, with shared board-specific clock configuration. The startup retains RMK’s peripheral assignments, MPSL task, controller memory sizing, RNG, regulator settings, and device-derived BLE address. The cost is maintaining that startup code alongside RMK updates. No new override API, and no global assumption that every nRF keyboard has a crystal.

I also updated to current upstream RMK with my tap-hold work and the USB readiness fix integrated. The final firmware does not include the latency-120 experiment: the normal host policy returns to latency 60. Both halves select their LF crystals, whereas the first crystal diagnostic changed only the central.

Those distinctions matter. The final image isn’t the 70.77 µA diagnostic with one more line changed.

The application commit contains the final setup. Its firmware and native checks pin the same RMK integration revision. The application still has 45 passing host tests, and both release images build. The current measurements are the physical check those tests can’t provide.

About a milliamp awake, 62 microamps asleep

The final chip_init image gave me 1.030 mA over the first 29 seconds of the CSV, then 62.07 µA over the 20.57 seconds starting at the 30-second mark. The transition was around 29.6–29.7 seconds into this recording.

The GUI selection is 62.42 µA over 20.51 seconds. The CSV calculation starts at a slightly different boundary and gives 62.07 µA over 20.57 seconds. Both say about 62 µA; neither is the whole recording’s 629.86 µA GUI average.

Awake, without typing. This includes the running firmware and display maintenance, not an isolated panel-supply measurement.

The previous integration image, before moving to the application’s chip-init override, measured 62.54 µA over its final 30 seconds. The two captures show no meaningful power regression from removing the new override idea.

What does that buy a 110 mAh battery?

Each half of my Urchin has a 110 mAh battery, not 100 mAh. I’ve added that clarification to the previous post too. The halves have separate batteries; I can’t pool 220 mAh and divide it by central current.

For a deliberately simple central-only, continuously sleeping estimate:

hours = nominal capacity in mAh / average current in mA
Central sleep currentIdeal runtime from 110 mAh
270–300 µA, previous non-subrated measurementsAbout 15–17 days
124 µA, initial subrating-enabled minuteAbout 37 days
62.07 µA, final connected-sleep windowAbout 74 days

From the old 270–300 µA range to roughly 62 µA, that’s 77–79% less sleep current, or about 4.3–4.8 times the ideal sleep-only runtime. From the initial 124 µA subrating capture, it’s approximately a halving of current and a doubling of that ideal runtime.

That’s not a promise of 74 days between charges. It excludes typing, reconnections, battery self-discharge, usable capacity lost to cutoff, temperature, and aging. These captures also span firmware revisions, so this isn’t a controlled attribution of the total gain to the crystal alone. The older 47 µA peripheral result isn’t a fresh measurement of the updated peripheral either.

The overnight arithmetic is less ambitious and more directly useful: eight hours at 270–300 µA costs 2.16–2.40 mAh on the central; eight hours at 62.07 µA costs about 0.50 mAh. Roughly 1.66–1.90 mAh saved per eight-hour night, without intentionally dropping either connection.

For a usage example, assume four hours per day at the measured 1.03 mA awake-idle current and twenty hours asleep. The new budget is about 5.36 mAh/day, or 20.5 ideal days. Holding that same awake current fixed but substituting the old 270–300 µA sleep current gives 10.9–11.6 ideal days. That’s roughly 1.8–1.9 times the runtime in that model.

Four hours of awake idle isn’t a measured typing day. I’m using the same awake budget on both sides to show how sleep consumption affects the result, not claiming I measured that daily autonomy.

There are still microamps left to explain

The remaining floor is around 29–30 µA, nearly half the final connected-sleep average. I haven’t isolated its components yet.

The display task stops periodic work and cuts panel power, but its SPIM2 driver remains alive. The battery ADC stops each conversion but remains configured between samples. Those are investigation targets, not established leaks. “Enabled” in a register isn’t enough to assign a current to it.

Battery sampling still happens every 30 seconds during sleep. An unchanged percentage isn’t republished, so that doesn’t imply a host notification every 30 seconds. Flexible application work could be batched or deferred, but the BLE links have negotiated timing. SDC/MPSL handles their scheduling and high-priority interrupts; Embassy also runs its low-priority processing. It isn’t just a radio peripheral doing everything independently of the CPU, and it isn’t an ordinary application timer I can move next to the battery sample.

Before changing more of that, I wanted the configuration that had already helped to live in the application and survive an ordinary build. It does.

The displays weren’t secretly eating milliamps. The crystal wasn’t missing. I just hadn’t asked the firmware to use it.

I’ll take this correction too.
